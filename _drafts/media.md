My media consumption habits are, relative to the average, fairly minimal. I am
not on social media; I do not consume any news source (be it a paper, website,
or TV); I don't cruise Youtube or "surf the web", as nerds of a certain vintage
once did. And yet I have found other ways to overwhelm myself.

Everyone knows the pithy stats at this point, but I'll repeat them briefly. The
average modern human encounters more information in a day than our ancestors
even 300 years ago would encounter in a lifetime. Some ungodly number of content
is uploaded to Youtube every hour.

This begs the question — what the hell is this doing to our brains?

Subjectively, I can tell you I feel a mild sense of overwhelm. I recall when I
first joined Twitter, way back in 2008-2009 (footnote: I'm unsure exactly when,
as I've since deleted that account. I simply remember the time of my life when I
joined). It didn't take long at that firehose to come to terms with the fact
that I could not conceivably drink all the water. I struggled with Pokémon
Syndrome: I had to read them all.

This habit formed earlier than Twitter, of course. If you remember "surfing the
web", you probably also remember RSS. Back in the days of the Blogosphere, you
would find someone's content you liked and subscribe to their blog using an RSS
reader such as Google Reader (R.I.P.). Now, you had your own personal set of 
subscriptions — a personal magazine, if you will. You could check your RSS reader
and new posts would automatically be aggregated into one location. This was early 
Web 2.0 at its finest, and this technology lives on, underpinning Podcast feeds 
today.

When you start out with a handful of subscriptions, it's easy to keep up with
them. Check your feeds every day, read the quick ones (i.e. webcomics) and defer
the long ones. Read the longform stuff when you have a bigger block of time. It
was easy to keep your RSS garden pruned.

But then your subscription count grows as more people start publishing. Bloggers
try to monetize, so they add advertisements. They discover this works best if
they have fresh content, so they start posting more frequently. The content
begins to be watered down, less sustaining. You're now swimming in a sea of
mediocre content, barely keeping your head above water. And the number of unread
items start piling up. No longer can you hit "RSS Feed Zero" — 10 unread, 20,
100, 1000. You have a nagging sensation from that unread number in the back of 
your mind, draining you whether you realize it or not.

Today, this phenomenon is still present, still essentially the same, and very
much accelerated and distributed. You don't just have blog posts — you have
podcasts, Youtube channels, Patreon, several social media platforms, email
newsletters, talking heads on TV. Hell, you can't even simply buy a product
online anymore without adding to your inbound load — you get several order 
status updates and then nagging emails, asking you to review the product you
purchased.

Overwhelmed yet? I know I am. So what do we do about it?

Well, I started the year with a new plan. I had a handful of podcasts that I
listened to daily. These were the short, informative type: The Indicator from
Planet Money, ShortCircuit, Science Quickly (formerly 60 Second Science - and
a "podcast" I used to listen to in Google Reader), etc. These, I told myself,
were generally informative, generally not mired in the current political 
zeitgeist, and generally useful. But with a handful of 5-15 minute podcasts
at 1.5x playback, I found myself saddled with a solid 40 minutes, give or take, 
to get through them every day. It came to a head when I went elk hunting last
year and came back to a week's worth of these things.

So I came up with this solution: unsubscribe from all of these podcasts, which I
had my player set to automatically download and enqueue. In their stead, I would
buy a single periodical like The Economist, Newsweek, or The New Yorker and read
it, cover to cover. I would then buy the current issue when I finished the first
one and repeat. I would *not* take out a subscription, only to let them pile up.
Dr. Dan, my political science professor in college, had us do this with
Newsweek (though, being college students, we had a subscription and were expected 
to read and discuss each issue).

It is now October — care to hazard a guess as to how many periodicals I have
been through? Go ahead...think about it. I'll wait.

One half. Or thereabouts. I purchased the 2025 outlook edition of The Economist,
and I made it about halfway through before it started collecting dust on my
shelf. It's still sitting there. When I can make time to read anything that's
*not* about certain animals liking or disliking certain foods (link to Dragons
Love Tacos here), I do not choose to read this material; I would rather lose
myself in a fictional realm or read something more directly practical
(_Stillness is Key_ was the last one in this genre) or in-depth (think
biographies or histories).

So...problem solved? I'm no longer forcibly inundating myself with these daily
podcasts nor am I forcing myself to read similar content.

But...the daily podcasts are not the only podcasts I consume. I also consume
normal longform podcasts, preferring the 2-3 hour style of Tim Ferriss, Andrew
Huberman, or Peter Attia. And so in my approximately 30 minutes of car time
every day running my kiddo(s) to/from school, I still listen. And these podcasts
give me actionable insights, things I want to incoporate into my life in some
way, be it a new exercise to add to my regimen or some more research to revise
any number of my protocols.

Now, to make it easy to take notes while listening and driving, I've been using
Snipd, an AI-powered podcast tool that will intelligently collect snips when you
press a single button. This is safe while driving, as it's a big-ass button on
Android Auto, and it is much easier than dictating a note to myself via the same
mechanism.

But if I listen to a podcast or two every week, I THEN have a pile of these
snips that I really should do something with. They're piling up, and I now feel
the same sense of overwhelm I did when my RSS reader started piling up unread
items. Only these are items I'm creating, distilling relevant things from my
listening.

Now that I have noticed this, I have a similar solution to my previous: once I
listen to a podcast episode, I cannot start another one until I go through the
snips from that episode and incoporate them into my life somehow. I think this
process will generally look like:

1. export the snips to Obsidian (this functionality is built into Snipd)
2. further distil the information I cared about from the snips
3. incoporate that information into my other notes in Obsidian

# Conscious Consumption

All of this boils down to one concept: conscious consumption. Whether we are
talking about physical consumption or digital consumption, we *must* be
conscious about what we are doing. This becomes more and more important as
content becomes more and more prolific, all of it vying for your time and
attention. 

If you are consuming to entertain yourself, great. Choose something,
determine how much time you want to spend with it, and follow through. Don't let
the algorithms draw you into the autoplay black hole. 

If you are consuming to learn, awesome. But make sure to process your 
insights. Study them, let them evolve in your mind. Spaced repetition is key to 
actually learning, so be sure to go back and review old material while 
incorporating new material.

Take control of your consumption back and give your brain a break.
